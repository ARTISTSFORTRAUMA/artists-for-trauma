Aaron Baker

The will to rise.An alchemist with an athletic outlook.

Design & photo Per BernalInterview Tattiahna Welsh

Aaron Baker on possibility, purpose, the power of the mind, and what we choose to make from the lives we are given.

Aaron learned early what it meant to exist at the edge of control. As a professional motocross athlete, he calculated risk at speed, adapted to changing conditions, and became, as he puts it, “comfortable being uncomfortable.”

Preparation mattered, but certainty was an illusion. Eventually, you had to commit.

“Comfortable being uncomfortable.”
Aaron Baker

1999The edge of control

In 1999, a motocross accident sent Aaron headfirst into the ground, fracturing his C4, 5, & 6 cervical vertebrae and leaving him paralyzed from the chin down. The physical conditions of his life changed, but the instincts of an athlete remained: adapt to what is in front of you and keep moving toward what might be possible.

TodayAn alchemist with an athletic outlook

Today, Aaron describes himself as an “alchemist with an athletic outlook.”

The alchemist is interested in transformation, in taking whatever material life presents and asking what else can be made from it. The athlete provides the method: study, repetition, adaptation, and the willingness to work without a guaranteed outcome.

“I like to look at possibility versus probability,” Aaron says. “I like to think that life happens for me, not to me. I’m not a victim of life. I choose.”

Then comes the tension at the center of that belief:

“Life is a dance between making it happen and allowing it to happen.”
Aaron Baker

The mindSix inches between my ears

For Aaron, the mind is its own kind of terrain. The discipline once required to compete became a way of approaching a body whose conditions had fundamentally changed.

He became a student of that body, learning its complications while looking for places where change might still be possible.

“The only difference between me and the fastest rider or the happiest person in the world is the six inches between my ears,” he says.

Thought cannot erase physical reality, but it can influence how we meet it. The mind is where Aaron locates agency: not in controlling the outcome, but in deciding how fully he will participate in pursuing it.

That distinction matters because Aaron’s worldview is not built around denying reality.

“I have a spinal cord injury. I have to accept that. I’m not satisfied with staying there.”
Aaron Baker

Acceptance gives him somewhere honest to begin. Curiosity keeps that starting point from becoming an ending.

Aaron can acknowledge what is true without assuming he knows everything about what comes next.

“Who knows what’s possible?”

FamilyStrength is never entirely solitary

Aaron did not develop that outlook alone. His mother, Laquita Dian, was beside him through the aftermath of his injury.

Later, Aaron and his mother became involved in building a rehabilitation, adaptive fitness, and wellness facility in Los Angeles, extending the lessons of his own recovery into a space centered on movement, wellness, and possibility.

Her role complements the familiar story of individual will. Aaron believes deeply in personal agency, but his life also demonstrates the importance of the people willing to remain beside us while that agency is being rebuilt.

Strength can be deeply personal without ever being entirely solitary.

Mind seedsBeing before doing

Aaron calls the ideas we plant in one another “mind seeds.”

He has distilled six of his own into a philosophy he calls the B.A.S.I.C.S. Method, grounded in a simple principle: Being before Doing.

The B.A.S.I.C.S. Method

The progression moves from the internal outward. Become present. Understand where you are. Accept what is in front of you. Choose how to meet it. Commit. Then share what you have learned.

B.A.S.I.C.S. is where Aaron’s philosophy becomes practice, a way of navigating uncertainty without pretending uncertainty can be eliminated.

CreationLife is a big canvas

That thinking also lives on the page. In his memoir, The Rebellious Recovery: Transform Your Adversity Into Adventure, Aaron gives language to ideas he has spent years testing against his own life.

Yet he is far more interested in creation than retrospection.

“I’m focused on creation, living forward, looking forward, building anew,” he says. “Life is a big canvas.”

The past provides material, but Aaron’s attention remains fixed on what can be created from it now.

Artists for TraumaAn unspoken understanding

That idea of creation is also where Aaron’s path intersects with Artists for Trauma (AFT) and its founder, Laura Sharpe. The two first connected through Aaron’s work in rehabilitation and adaptive fitness.

An AFT pop-up studio and project soon followed. Aaron remembers an immediate “kinship” and “unspoken understanding” between them, the beginning of a collaboration that has continued for years.

Today, Aaron serves on AFT’s Board of Directors. The connection makes sense within Aaron’s larger philosophy. Laura built AFT around creative expression and human connection after life-altering trauma; Aaron was exploring many of those same questions through the body and mind.

Their approaches were different, but their instinct was similar: Trauma may change the conditions of a life without having to determine the limits of one.

Art made that intersection personal. While learning to write with his left hand, he began painting, later exhibiting his work. Creativity was another way of exploring adaptation: an idea beginning in the mind and moving through the body.

That philosophy extends through his foundation, Adversity into Adventure, which expands access to rehabilitation, recreation, and neurological research, an approach it calls its “3R Ethos.”

That mission intersects with AFT as the two are now turning a custom wheelchair into a work of art, fusing mobility, identity, and creative expression.

“Life is a big canvas.”
Aaron Baker

Aaron believes that it is another form of alchemy: not erasing what an object is, but expanding what it can represent.

There is an urgency underneath his curiosity and he says:

“Apathy in life accelerates entropy.”
Aaron Baker

The idea reveals a sharper edge to his philosophy: movement matters. Not because every effort guarantees progress, but because disengaging from your own life carries its own consequence.

For Aaron, there remains a “fierce rage against the pain,” a reason to continue pushing simply because he can.

That outlook continues through his work with Adversity Into Adventure, Permobil, Wings for Life, and several other organizations that he serves.

Beyond himselfHow many people can I affect?

It also exists within the life he has built with his wife, Katelyn, and their daughter.

Aaron is still moving, but increasingly the question is what that movement can create for people beyond himself.

He asks himself, “Am I satisfied with my effort?”

But his measure of a meaningful life increasingly extends beyond himself: “How many people can I affect?”

That may be where the alchemist and athlete ultimately meet. Experience is transformed by what we do with it, but its meaning grows when it moves beyond us.

Aaron puts it another way:

“Inspiration is a wheel. What I give you, you give me, and then we give others.”
Aaron Baker

It is also what makes Aaron’s connection with Laura Sharpe and Artists for Trauma feel less like a partnership than an extension of the way he already sees the world: Creation becomes connection, connection becomes service, and what one person discovers can become a possibility for someone else.

The will to rise begins within the individual. Its meaning grows in what we give once we do.

“Who knows what’s possible?”
Aaron Baker
