AFT & Laura Sharpe's Artists Profile

Risky

keep on keepin onKelly GravalWest Coast Writing Pioneer

Cover Photo & Design Per Bernal

A pioneering West Coast graffiti writer painting Los Angeles since 1983, Kelly “Risk” Graval built his name by adapting a New York culture to a city without subways.

Freight trains, freeways, & overpasses became moving canvases.

That same work eventually brought him into the international collector world, where conversations begin with the old masters and Risk is introduced as the contemporary artist in the room.

“I guess I’m an artist that will never know completion. I just do what I do & love what I do, so I just keep doing it.”
Kelly Graval

1983“What do you write?”

It started in 1983, in a West LA classroom, with a wave drawn on a desk. A kid from New York leaned over and asked Kelly, “What do you write?”

That night, he stole spray paint, jumped a fence, and put up his first piece. It was bad. The next day, a big crowd had gathered around it, admiring it. Kelly was hooked. He has not stopped painting since.

The workRepetition and discipline

The work was never about recognition. It was about repetition, discipline, and the act itself, a medium applied to a surface until it becomes second nature.

Kelly committed fully, pushing graffiti beyond subculture and toward something that could stand on its own as art. He wasn’t trying to leave graffiti behind. He was trying to expand what it could be.

HollywoodReal graffiti, not hairspray

In the early years, Kelly inserted himself into spaces that weren’t built for him. He walked onto film sets and pointed out the difference between real graffiti and what crews were faking with hairspray.

If there was no budget, he painted it himself for food, trading access for opportunity and building a reputation by showing up and doing the work.

1987“We own it now.”

That hustle led to a call from Michael Jackson’s team. Surrounded by resources Kelly had never seen before, he watched director Joe Pytka take a sledgehammer to a car on set just to get the shot, while commenting, “We own it now.”

Watching that vision come to life in real time during the filming of “The Way You Make Me Feel” became a turning point. It was the moment Kelly decided to take both his life and his art seriously.

Color fieldBeyond the letters

At a certain point, the name, Risk, was no longer the point. It had already carried its weight. Kelly started focusing more on color, scale, and the feeling of the work, how it hits you immediately.

He wasn’t leaving graffiti behind, just expanding past lettering. The work opened up into something more immediate, where nothing needs to be read to be understood.

That shift led to color field murals, where color itself becomes the message.

Artists for TraumaLess explaining, more feeling

Without text or symbols, the work relies on scale, composition, and instinct to create an immediate emotional response. It is less about explaining and more about feeling, the same impact graffiti had on the street, translated into a different form.

Through Kelly’s work with Artists for Trauma and his connection with Laura Sharpe, that understanding expanded beyond traditional art spaces. The work moved into hospitals & rehabilitation centers, existing as part of the environment rather than separate from it, offering something immediate and human in places that often feel clinical.

RecoveryA simulator, transformed

A rehabilitation driving simulator, once an institutional object, becomes something entirely different when approached through art.

Through color, movement, & presence, the simulator is transformed into an experience that feels alive, creating a moment of community and connection within a setting designed for recovery.

The compoundBuilt for sharing

That same philosophy carries into the studio he has built over time. What began as a personal workspace has grown into a compound, designed not just for making work but for sharing it.

Open to artists, students, & collaborators, including those navigating physical challenges, it reflects the idea that art should be accessible, communal, and continuously evolving.

“This was always meant to be not just for me.”
Kelly Graval

What Kelly has built goes beyond the work itself. It lives in the spaces he has created, the artists he has brought in, and the way the art form continues to move forward without him needing to define it.

“I just want the art form to keep moving.”
Kelly Graval
