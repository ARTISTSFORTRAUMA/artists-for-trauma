The Art of +Healing

How the healing journey extends beyond the operating roomDr. Thomas K. DuncanD.O., FACS, FICS

Photo & Design Per BernalInterview Tattiahna Welsh

Every trauma surgeon remembers certain patients. Some because of the injuries they survived. Others because of the lessons they left behind.

For more than two decades, Doctor Thomas K. Duncan has cared for patients at the most critical moments of their lives. As Trauma Medical Director at Ventura County Medical Center and Medical Co-Director of Anacapa Surgical Associates, he has learned that survival is only the beginning.

While surgery can repair the body, true healing requires hope, purpose, and the opportunity to rediscover who you are after trauma. That belief ultimately led him to Artists for Trauma (AFT), where healing continues long after a patient leaves the operating room.

“True healing requires hope, purpose & the opportunity to rediscover who you are after trauma.”
Dr. Thomas K. Duncan

Nigeria, Ghana & ChicagoHealing begins with compassion

Before becoming a surgeon, Dr. Duncan learned that healing begins with compassion. Raised between Nigeria and Ghana, he was inspired by his parents’ commitment to serving others and by a close childhood friend living with sickle cell disease.

Watching his friend struggle through painful complications taught him that illness affects every part of a person’s life. Those experiences shaped not only the physician he would become, but also the determination that carried him through his own journey.

After immigrating to the United States from Nigeria at age seventeen, Dr. Duncan had to repeat classes in Chicago as many of his credits did not transfer. There, he excelled academically, giving him the confidence to continue pursuing a career in medicine. He went on to earn his bachelor’s degree in Biology at Chicago State University, before receiving his Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine degree from the Chicago College of Osteopathic Medicine.

“Every shift brought victims of gun violence, stabbings & severe injuries through the trauma bay.”
Dr. Thomas K. Duncan

King/Drew Medical CenterOne unforgettable shift

Dr. Duncan completed his surgical residency at King/Drew Medical Center in Los Angeles during the height of gang violence in the 1990’s, where every shift brought victims of gun violence, stabbings, and severe injuries through the trauma bay.

The experience raised a question that would shape the rest of his career: How could healthcare help prevent violence before another patient reached the operating room?

The answer came during one unforgettable overnight shift. After his team lost a young gunshot victim despite every effort, the man’s twin brother quietly told him:

“I know who did this to my brother. You should be expecting more business.”

“It was so heartbreaking,” Dr. Duncan recalled. In that moment, he realized they were treating the consequences of violence while communities remained trapped in the cycle that caused it.

“In that moment, he realized they were treating the consequences of violence.”
Dr. Thomas K. Duncan

Artists for TraumaPrevention, partnership & community

His mission expanded beyond emergency surgery to help prevent trauma before it happened. The lesson from that night transformed Dr. Duncan’s approach to medicine. Healing, he realized, required more than surgery. It depended on prevention, partnership and community.

That philosophy eventually led him to Laura Sharpe, Founder of Artists for Trauma. Introduced through Ventura County Medical Center Foundation leader Amy Towner, Dr. Duncan immediately recognized that Laura and AFT addressed a part of recovery he had seen medicine alone could not always reach.

Through creativity, survivors found confidence, connection, and a renewed sense of purpose. “It takes a village to make an organization function,” Dr. Duncan said.

“It takes a village to make an organization function.”
Dr. Thomas K. Duncan

Onofre BanderasFrom the trauma bay to the studio

One of the clearest examples of that philosophy is artist and trauma survivor Onofre “Ono” Banderas.

While on call, Dr. Duncan received word that a young man with a gunshot wound to the gut was being rushed to Ventura County Medical Center. By the time Ono arrived in the trauma bay, the trauma team was ready. Within minutes, Dr. Duncan determined Ono needed immediate surgery and took him to the operating room. Ono was conscious but drifting in and out as they prepared him for surgery.

As Ono recovered in the hospital, Dr. Duncan learned about his passion for art. Believing creativity could become an important part of his healing, he introduced Ono to Laura Sharpe and AFT, connecting him with a community that could support his recovery beyond the hospital walls.

Watching Ono turning into a respected artist and community leader reinforced Dr. Duncan’s belief that purpose can be one of recovery’s greatest gifts.

“It’s also great to have an aspect of life to focus on, & not be borne down with the burden of recovering.”
Dr. Thomas K. Duncan

Camp HOPEAnother avenue to heal

His commitment to healing eventually extended to children. Through his leadership with the Ventura County Family Justice Center Foundation, Dr. Duncan introduced AFT to Camp HOPE, a program serving children and teens affected by domestic violence, abuse, and other traumatic experiences.

Recognizing that many young survivors struggle to express emotions through words alone, he saw an opportunity to unite two organizations with a shared mission. By bringing AFT to Camp HOPE, children gained another avenue to heal through creativity, build confidence, and discover that their experiences do not define their future.

For Dr. Duncan, art is more than creative expression. It is another form of healing. “When there’s psychological trauma, having that ability to use a different part of your brain is great,” he explained. “If you don’t have anything to focus on other than the trauma, it could take you down a rabbit hole... to have an avenue or egress... is a way to keep people alive.”

To him, recovery is about more than surviving an injury. It is about rediscovering purpose, reconnecting with others, and believing in a future beyond trauma.

“If you don’t have anything to focus on other than the trauma, it could take you down a rabbit hole...”
Dr. Thomas K. Duncan

The messagePatience & hope

Whether caring for patients, mentoring physicians, or supporting survivors through Artists for Trauma, Dr. Duncan returns to the same message: healing requires patience and hope.

“The pot of gold is at the end of the rainbow,” he said. “Don’t get discouraged. Sometimes it takes patience and resilience to get there.” It is advice he hopes every survivor carries with them as they move forward.

Throughout his career, he has believed that lasting recovery is built through collaboration. Physicians, artists, advocates, educators, volunteers, and families each play a role in helping survivors heal. For him, the work does not end when an operation is over. In many ways, that is where it truly begins.

“Don’t get discouraged. Sometimes it takes patience and resilience to get there.”
Dr. Thomas K. Duncan

Off dutyA DJ at heart

Away from the hospital, Dr. Duncan enjoys spending time with his wife and their two daughters.

Another lifelong passion offers a glimpse into how he approaches both medicine and life: music. If he had not become a physician, he says he probably would have been a DJ.

Music is always playing in his operating room. One moment it might be hip-hop; the next, jazz, classical, or even Baby Einstein. “It’s like a box of chocolates,” he joked. “You never know what you’re going to get.”

When he listens, he doesn’t simply hear the song. He listens to the percussion, the bass, the guitar, and every instrument individually, appreciating how each part contributes to the whole. “I break down the music like I break down aspects of my clinical care.”

Throughout his career, Dr. Duncan remained focused not only on saving lives but also on helping survivors rebuild their lives.

“What ties it all together for me is making sure that my patient gets to a good space and survives.”
Dr. Thomas K. Duncan

It is a philosophy that extends far beyond the operating room, reminding us that while surgery can save a life, compassion, creativity, and community help make it whole.

That is the true art of healing.
