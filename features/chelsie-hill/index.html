AFT & Laura Sharpe's Artists Profile

Chelsie Hill

Creating Space.

Cover & design Per BernalInterview Tattiahna Welsh

Chelsie on dance, advocacy, representation, and creating a community where no one has to explain who they are.

Dance has been the constant in Chelsie Hill’s life, even when everything else changed. She started at three and was competing by five.

She grew up in Monterey, CA, immersed in rehearsals, conventions, competitions, and the sisterhood she found among dancers.

MontereyA future built on dance

Chelsie knew where she wanted dance to take her: Los Angeles, where she imagined becoming a professional backup dancer. Dance was where Chelsie felt most herself, and the future she pictured was inseparable from it.

At seventeen, a life-altering accident changed the future she had dreamed of. She got into a car with a friend who had been drinking. They hit a tree head-on, leaving Chelsie with a broken back, paralyzed from the waist down.

When a doctor told her she was paralyzed, Chelsie’s mind went immediately to the identity she had carried since childhood. Chelsie had hardly any time to process what it meant to lose the ability to walk; she could only think about what it meant for her ability to dance.

She spent 51 days in the hospital with few role models of young wheelchair users building careers, families, or futures she could recognize. Even as she struggled to accept her new reality, dance was still her priority.

Two weeks after returning home, her father sourced wheelchairs for her high school dance team. Her teammates learned to dance in the chairs, and Chelsie, still wearing her back brace, performed alongside them.

“That’s when I realized, oh, that fire is still in me.”
Chelsie Hill

Auti AngelAnother source of direction

That drive found another source of direction through wheelchair dancer Auti Angel, whom Chelsie had first seen in a Snoop Dogg music video that her father showed her while she was hospitalized.

When they later met, Chelsie told Auti that she used to be a dancer. Auti stopped her.

“Once a dancer, always a dancer.”

“That one sentence altered my entire world,” Chelsie remembers.

AdvocacyMaking it personal

The accident also gave Chelsie a responsibility she continues to carry. As a passenger in a drunk-driving crash, Chelsie has made prevention part of her advocacy, speaking to high school and college students about drunk and distracted driving.

She remembers being young and feeling invincible; now, she sits before students and makes the consequences personal.

“I was just like you. I was a dancer. I was good in school. I did all these things, and I became paralyzed, and my entire life was altered.”

Her message holds two truths at once: one decision can change a life, and that changed life can still become something extraordinary.

2012Women who already understood

Rollettes began with Chelsie wanting to meet women who understood her life. In 2012, she reached out on Instagram and invited six women to Monterey. They danced, went to dinner, and moved through her hometown together, experiencing something deceptively simple: the ease of being with women who already understood.

For once, the details of life in a wheelchair required no explanation. That feeling stayed with Chelsie and became the foundation for a community where women with disabilities could enter, already knowing they belonged.

Los AngelesCreating what was missing

When Chelsie moved to Los Angeles in 2014, she entered the dance world she had dreamed of since childhood, taking classes with able-bodied dancers, learning choreography, and adapting the movement to spaces that had not been designed with her in mind.

Online, she saw the same absence. Wheelchair dancers were rarely seen in the professional dance content she watched, so Chelsie began creating what she and her friends could not find.

The ambition became larger than getting Chelsie into the room. She was beginning to build a room of her own. And women came.

By 2017, Rollettes Experience had grown to 30 women from three countries. A year later, attendance jumped to 118. It was then that Chelsie understood what had formed around her.

“This is so much bigger than me. This is a movement.”
Chelsie Hill

“This is a community of women that need each other and need a sisterhood.”

Dance remained at its center, but Rollettes Experience expanded around it to include education, advocacy, accessibility, wellness, mentorship, and conversations about navigating life with a disability.

Rollettes ExperienceBuild the door

The choreography brought them together, but the community gave them a reason to return.

As Rollettes grew, Chelsie still had to convince others that what she was building mattered. Early on, she remembers pitching companies that described Rollettes Experience as “cute,” unable to see its potential on a broader scale.

Instead of waiting for the industry to understand her vision, Chelsie created the proof herself. Her philosophy became simple:

“Build it, and they will come. Build the door, and they’ll go through it.”
Chelsie Hill

ParisA larger stage

Fourteen years after that first gathering in Monterey, the rooms are considerably larger. But Chelsie’s approach remains the same.

“If the space you need doesn’t exist, create it.”
Chelsie Hill

In 2024, that journey took Chelsie to Paris, where she performed in the Opening Ceremony of the Paralympic Games.

Artists for TraumaThe first to say yes

Laura Sharpe, founder of Artists for Trauma (AFT), saw what Chelsie was building early on in 2014. Chelsie still remembers Laura’s words:

“I love what you’re doing. I want to support it. I want to get involved.”

Laura helped cover the cost of the studio space for one of the early Rollettes camps, making AFT the first organization to sponsor them. Chelsie still sees that early support in how far Rollettes has come.

Shortly after their collaboration began, AFT sponsored the Rollettes at a 2015 Special Olympics Host Town Event, hosted by Kaiser Permanente Woodland Hills, Valley Cultural Center, and Macy’s.

Their partnership has continued to grow over the years, most recently at the 2026 Rollettes Experience at the Burbank Marriott Convention Center, where AFT returned with an interactive art workshop on July 24, bringing hands-on creative expression to a weekend centered on dance, education, empowerment, and community.

2026The room is getting bigger

2026 also marks another chapter of growth for Rollettes Experience, as the community expands beyond its Los Angeles home with an experience in Houston.

What began with a small gathering of women in Monterey is now reaching new cities, giving more women and children with disabilities the opportunity to find the connection Chelsie first discovered more than a decade ago.

For AFT, the continued collaboration reflects the same principle that has connected Laura and Chelsie from the beginning: creativity can become a point of connection after a life-altering experience.

Years after Laura first helped secure a studio for a small gathering of women, AFT remains part of the community that grew from it.

The room has changed. Now, it is getting bigger.

TodayHeld the door open

Today, Chelsie measures what she built less by how many people fill the room than by what happens once they enter it. She watches girls arrive without knowing anyone and leave with friends.

Younger attendees meet women navigating careers, relationships, motherhood, and adulthood with disabilities, examples Chelsie herself struggled to see at seventeen.

“Rollettes Experience isn’t for me anymore. It’s for all of the people that attend every year.”
Chelsie Hill

Now a mother herself, Chelsie is raising a daughter who will grow up seeing disability, leadership, dance, and community not as exceptions, but as part of everyday life.

It is a future Chelsie once struggled to see for herself, now made visible for the next generation.

Chelsie did not ask to fit into a room that already existed. She made her own.

And then she held the door open.
